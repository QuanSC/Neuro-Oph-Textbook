Anisocoria

Physiologic, pharmacologic, Horner syndrome, third nerve palsy, tonic pupil and light-near dissociation. Built for second-year residents.

Step by step

  1. Look for danger first: ptosis with ophthalmoplegia (CN III), pain, or neurologic symptoms. A pupil-involving CN III palsy needs emergent vascular imaging; an acute painful Horner needs emergent head and neck vascular imaging.
  2. Measure in bright and dim light. If anisocoria is greater in dim light, the small pupil is abnormal (sympathetic problem or physiologic). If greater in bright light, the large pupil is abnormal (parasympathetic, iris or drug).
  3. Small pupil abnormal: look for mild ptosis, lower-lid elevation and dilation lag. Confirm Horner with apraclonidine (reversal) or cocaine (post-cocaine anisocoria of 0.8 mm or more). Localize with associated signs and imaging; hydroxyamphetamine separates postganglionic from more proximal lesions.
  4. Large pupil abnormal: check motility and lids for CN III; slit lamp for sphincter tears, synechiae or sectoral palsy with vermiform movements; test near response.
  5. Dilute pilocarpine (0.1%) constricts a tonic pupil (denervation supersensitivity). Pilocarpine 1% constricts any pupil except one with pharmacologic blockade or iris damage.
  6. Both pupils react poorly to light but well to near: light-near dissociation: dorsal midbrain, Argyll Robertson pupils, bilateral tonic pupils, diabetes, or severe bilateral afferent loss.
Remember
About 19% of normal people show 0.4 mm or more of anisocoria at any given exam (Lam 1987). Physiologic anisocoria stays roughly equal in light and dark, with brisk reactions and no ptosis.

Where the numbers come from

  • Post-cocaine anisocoria of 0.8 mm gave a mean odds ratio of about 1050:1 for Horner syndrome (lower 95% confidence limit about 37:1); absolute post-cocaine anisocoria predicted better than the change in anisocoria: Kardon RH, Denison CE, Brown CK, Thompson HS. Critical evaluation of the cocaine test in the diagnosis of Horner's syndrome. Arch Ophthalmol. 1990;108(3):384-387. doi:10.1001/archopht.1990.01070050082036.
  • About 19% of normal people show anisocoria of 0.4 mm or more at any given exam: Lam BL, Thompson HS, Corbett JJ. The prevalence of simple anisocoria. Am J Ophthalmol. 1987;104(1):69-73.
  • Only 36% of patients with acute third nerve palsy received timely arterial imaging: Chung JE, Schroeder RM, Wilson B, Van Stavern GP, Stunkel L. Failure to obtain urgent arterial imaging in acute third nerve palsies. J Neuroophthalmol. 2021. doi:10.1097/WNO.0000000000001337.
  • Apraclonidine is contraindicated in young children (use cocaine instead): EyeWiki, Anisocoria (American Academy of Ophthalmology). Accessed 2026.
  • Pupil sizes in the lab are illustrative values, not measurements from patients. Other content reflects standard teaching in the core texts.
